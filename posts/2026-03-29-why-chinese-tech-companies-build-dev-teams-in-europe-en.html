Why Chinese Tech Companies Have Software Development Teams in Europe

Earlier this year, I kept coming back to one question: why do some Chinese tech companies set up software development teams in Europe?

For major Chinese internet and tech companies such as Tencent, Alibaba, ByteDance, and Xiaomi, having offices in Europe is nothing unusual. But those offices usually do not do what I would consider real software development. That is actually not hard to understand. Software development is not generally seen as one of Europe’s strengths. It often takes longer, yet the output and efficiency are usually hard to compare with development teams in China. As a result, most tech companies in Europe mainly maintain teams focused on localization, legal affairs, compliance, sales, and government relations, rather than relocating software development itself. This is not unique to Chinese companies. American companies often do the same, for much the same reason.

Later on, however, I noticed that some Chinese companies, and even some American ones, especially those involved in hardware, telecommunications, cloud services, and infrastructure, maintain a considerable number of software teams in Europe. I do not know enough about the American cases to comment on them here. But in the case of one particular Chinese company, at least from what I have seen, its teams in Germany and elsewhere in Europe have produced almost nothing that looks like meaningful R&D output. In fact, a handful of employees from China could probably replace an entire local team. That was what puzzled me for so long: if there is no obvious output, what is the point of maintaining such a team? I spent months thinking about this, and only recently did I feel that I had finally understood it.

If you look at the issue purely from the perspective of software development, I do not see much direct value in placing software teams in Germany or Europe. The point is not really to write better software, or to write it faster. TSMC is a useful comparison. Building fabs in Europe is far more expensive, slower, and less efficient than doing so in Taiwan. But that decision was never purely about business logic. It was also shaped by geopolitics and industrial strategy. I think the same logic applies to Chinese companies setting up software development teams in Germany and Europe.

In many cases, the purpose of these teams is not R&D output as such, but politics and market access. This is especially true for companies facing clear security concerns and political pressure in the European market. They need to present themselves as companies that are genuinely rooted locally: companies with local technical capabilities, a long-term commitment, engineering and delivery capacity, strong ties to the local industrial ecosystem, partnerships with universities and research institutions, and the ability to create local jobs. In other words, the main reason these teams exist is often not development efficiency, but market access.

To put it more plainly, these software teams are often not where the company’s core R&D actually happens. They are better understood as a form of market-access R&D. They may take on some localization work, testing and validation, customer support, and engineering coordination, but more importantly, they help demonstrate to customers, governments, and partners that the company is not simply an outsider trying to sell products. It is a company willing to invest locally over the long term, support employment, and integrate itself into the local industrial ecosystem.

Seen this way, the existence of these software teams in Europe cannot be judged simply by their R&D output. Their real significance lies less in technical results than in market access, political risk management, and the building of local trust. What may look like an inefficient organizational setup is not necessarily a management failure. In some cases, it may actually be the most realistic choice in a particular external environment. Once I started looking at it this way, a lot of things that had seemed puzzling suddenly made sense.

Still, I sometimes wonder whether this strategy will continue to work in the future. Geopolitical, ideological, and broader value conflicts are all becoming more intense. From my own experience living in Europe, many decisions here today do not seem to be based entirely on facts or practical interests, but are increasingly shaped by ideology, values, and political considerations. To be honest, I do not fully understand this logic, because in my view it does not necessarily serve a country’s long-term development, nor does it genuinely serve the interests of its people. More importantly, on many key issues Europe does not seem to have full strategic autonomy. If one day the United States were to press Europe to completely exclude a particular Chinese company, Europe might not have much room to maneuver. So I cannot really say how effective this strategy of exchanging local investment for market access will remain in the future. It may slow down the process of being pushed out of the market and make that process more difficult, but it may not be enough to change the final outcome. Ultimately, a society that becomes increasingly detached from facts and increasingly driven by ideology and value judgments is, in my view, heading toward a more uncertain future.
