Why Alibaba Is a Unique Case in the AI Era

I’ve been thinking about a question lately: how is it that a company like Alibaba, with complex operations, heavy legacy, and layered organization, can still stay at the forefront of foundation models, even after its founder, Jack Ma, stepped away from day-to-day management? The more I think about it, the less straightforward it becomes.

At its core, building foundation models is not something large companies are naturally good at. It is a long and difficult game. It requires sustained investment, tolerance for uncertainty, and the ability to push forward even when returns are unclear. More importantly, it requires technical teams to operate without being tightly constrained by existing business logic. In other words, this is not something that can be achieved through normal corporate execution.

From this perspective, foundation models tend to be dominated by two types of companies. The first are startups. They are focused, lightweight, and free from legacy constraints. Founders can commit fully, and teams can move quickly around a single goal. Companies like OpenAI, Anthropic, xAI, DeepSeek, Moonshot, MiniMax, and Z.ai fall into this category. What they have in common is that their existence is tightly tied to this technological frontier. The second type are large companies driven by strong founder intent. These companies do not accelerate in AI because the organization naturally finds the right direction, but because founders step in and enforce alignment on resources, priorities, and pace. Even without clear short-term returns, they can push forward. ByteDance, Google, and Xiaomi all show this pattern as AI becomes central again.

Alibaba fits neither category. It is not a startup but a mature company with complex structure, high coordination costs, and strong internal inertia. Any new strategic direction faces resistance by default. At the same time, its founder is no longer involved in daily operations.

And yet, under these conditions, Alibaba has built one of the most prominent foundation model families globally. The more important question is not why Qwen is strong, but how Alibaba continues to produce Qwen over time. Because building one strong model is not the hardest part. The real challenge is whether a large company can avoid falling behind over multiple years, keep pushing its models forward, and do so without being pulled back by its existing business structure.

Alibaba has done exactly that, and that is the real point. If you look closely, the people who brought Alibaba back onto this path were not Jack Ma, but Joe Tsai and Eddie Wu. This is key to understanding what makes Alibaba different. On the surface, the founder has stepped away. But in reality, the company has not fully transitioned into a typical professionally managed organization. Both Tsai and Wu are part of the founding layer. Strategic control has not completely left that group. This makes Alibaba different from companies that rely entirely on professional managers. More importantly, they did not simply take over, they redefined the company’s direction. They made AI a core strategy, not just in words but in real resource allocation, organizational focus, and long-term positioning.

But that alone is not enough to explain the outcome. Making AI a top-level strategy explains why Alibaba is willing to invest, but not why it can consistently deliver strong models. The more critical factor is that, despite being a large company, Alibaba has managed to preserve elements of a startup within its model efforts. This is the difficult part. It does not mean Alibaba has become a startup. It means that, in this domain, it operates like one in key ways: clear focus, high priority for technical teams, fast iteration, continuous releases, and insulation from short-term financial pressure and internal complexity.

This matters because competition in foundation models is not only about parameters, data, or compute, but about organizational dynamics. Many large companies struggle not because they lack resources, but because their structures dilute long-term, high-uncertainty efforts. Processes increase, coordination becomes heavier, priorities scatter. Projects continue, but the sense of urgency disappears.

What is unusual about Alibaba is that it combines the resources of a large company with the execution style of a startup. It is not running a typical big-company AI initiative. Instead, it has maintained an internal environment where startup-like dynamics can survive. That is why Qwen does not peak once and fade. It keeps iterating, keeps releasing, and keeps improving. Its pace reflects a team that still operates at the frontier.

Once AI is elevated to a core strategy and this execution model is preserved, Alibaba’s structural advantages begin to compound. It is not just building models. It also has cloud infrastructure, enterprise customers, real-world use cases, platforms, and a developer ecosystem. In other words, Alibaba is not making a single bet on one model. It is using the full resource base of a large company to amplify a team that still operates with startup DNA.

This is very different from other companies. Tencent has resources and distribution, but its Hunyuan models have not entered the top tier. Baidu started early, but over the past decade has often moved early without securing the final position. Microsoft is strong overall, but its in-house models have yet to clearly prove top-tier capability. Meta was at the forefront with Llama 3, but by Llama 4 it has largely been pushed out of the leading group. Huawei followed a similar path. Its Pangu models were competitive in 2024, but quickly lost visibility. By 2025, it was even at one point accused of “wrapping Qwen.” Taken together, this suggests that Huawei has clearly fallen behind in sustaining foundation model development, and calls into question its ability to independently develop such models.

What makes Alibaba unique is that it is neither a startup built solely for foundation models nor a company driven by a returning founder. And yet it has built a genuinely first-tier model capability. What is interesting is not that Alibaba breaks the rule, but that it represents a variation of it. Typically, success in foundation models comes either from startup-level focus or from direct founder intervention. Alibaba follows neither path. Instead, it retains founder-level strategic control, elevates AI to the main trajectory of the company, leverages cloud, ecosystem, and enterprise demand, and maintains an internal team that still operates with startup DNA. The result is a rare structure: a large company using its resource density to support a team that moves like a startup.

This is enough to explain Alibaba’s position in this AI era. What is truly scarce is not recognizing the importance of AI. Everyone does. What is scarce is whether a large company can sustain long-term commitment, tolerate uncertainty, and move with startup-level speed without the founder returning. That is where Alibaba is different.

Alibaba, at least so far, has done it.
