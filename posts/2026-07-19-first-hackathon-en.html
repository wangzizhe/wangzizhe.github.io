My First Hackathon

This weekend, I took part in my first-ever hackathon. I had never been particularly interested in hackathons before because I always felt that my programming skills were not strong enough to turn an idea into something real. Over the past few years, however, my curiosity has grown, and with the help of AI agents, I no longer need to spend as much time worrying about the technical details of implementation. Instead, I can focus more on the problem itself: Is this a real problem? What solutions already exist? What are users actually struggling with? And which specific angle could I approach it from? I also wanted to use the hackathon as an opportunity to learn new things quickly. Working alongside people from different countries and backgrounds would allow me to see how others think, while also helping me understand my own strengths and weaknesses more clearly. I also assumed that people who enjoy hackathons are generally curious and eager to explore new ideas. Even if I did not win anything, meeting interesting people would still make the experience worthwhile. On top of that, it took place over the weekend, so it did not interfere with my regular schedule. It seemed like a very good deal.

So I signed up. At the beginning, though, I was not seriously thinking about winning. Since it was my first hackathon, I mainly wanted to understand how the whole process worked and pick up some free API credits for my own projects. I chose to attend the local hub in Dresden. At 3 p.m. on Saturday, I arrived at the venue, YETI. At 6 p.m., all the local hubs and online participants joined a Zoom call for the official kickoff session. To be honest, this part was incredibly boring. The session lasted around 50 minutes, and apart from the final two slides, there was almost nothing useful in it. After each hub briefly said hello, most of the remaining time was essentially devoted to sponsor promotion. The first speaker was the CEO of Maschmeyer Group, who appeared in a prerecorded video. The production quality was so poor that I had no idea who had edited it. It looked completely AI-generated, and honestly, it was worse than many AI-generated videos I have seen. I barely paid attention to the rest of the session because there was not much substance, and the microphone quality at one of the hubs was so bad that listening to them speak was genuinely painful. At 6:50 p.m., the organizers finally revealed the challenges. Those ten minutes were probably the only genuinely useful part of the entire hour. Once the challenges had been announced, we had 20 hours to build our projects, with a submission deadline of 3 p.m. on Sunday. The hackathon had officially begun. After receiving 50 dollars in OpenAI API credits, I chatted with a few people at the venue and then went home.

The next morning, I gave the whole thing some more thought. My original plan had been to use this event simply to learn how hackathons worked, and then take another one more seriously in October. But then I thought, since I had already signed up, why not actually take part? There were only five hours left before the deadline, but five hours was still five hours. I decided to build whatever I could in the time I had. At the very least, completing a project would give me a much deeper understanding of the process and help me assess the gap between myself and the other participants more accurately. Out of the six challenges, I chose the one I found most interesting, Challenge 2: how to identify promising founders more quickly and accurately, and invest in them. I liked the idea because it gave me an opportunity to look at myself from a different perspective, this time through the eyes of an investor.

By 2:30 p.m., the project itself was mostly finished, but I still had to record three separate videos. By the time I was done, it was already 2:50. I started the submission process and discovered that I also had to answer a long list of questions, including what market gap the product addressed, who the target users were, and what its unique selling proposition was. I finished filling everything out at 2:58 and clicked submit, only to be told that I first needed to complete my profile. I rushed to update it, checked the time, and saw that it was already 2:59. I immediately clicked submit again. Fortunately, it went through.

Now I just have to wait a week for the results. I am curious to see what the other participants built, and I hope to learn from the strongest projects. I also discovered that the 50 dollars in OpenAI API credits covered roughly 50 million tokens. Based on my usual usage of around 200 million tokens per day, using the API at that rate would cost me about 6,000 dollars a month. So apparently, OpenAI would have a very hard time making money from me.

Overall, it was a very positive experience. Next time, however, I will probably participate entirely online. That would allow me to skip the largely pointless one-hour kickoff session, and my home setup is better suited to the way I work and makes it easier for me to stay focused. I also do not think there is any need to stay up all night. For me, the best way to approach a hackathon is to eat normally, sleep normally, and treat it as something enjoyable. It is an opportunity to learn new things, expose yourself to new ideas, and discover your own strengths and weaknesses, rather than simply treating it as a competition.
