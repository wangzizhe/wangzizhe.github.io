China Achieves Successful Rocket Recovery

“On July 10, 2026, the Long March 10B carrier rocket completed its successful maiden flight from the Hainan Commercial Space Launch Site. Its first stage was precisely captured on an offshore recovery platform using a world first, a high strength arresting net arranged in a grid pattern, together with a hook and cable mechanism mounted on the rocket. The achievement marks China’s official entry into the era of reusable rockets.”

I was incredibly excited when I saw the footage of the successful recovery. China now has this capability as well, and it has chosen a technical approach that is very different from SpaceX’s. SpaceX’s Falcon 9 lands vertically using deployable landing legs, while the Long March 10B eliminates the landing legs and is instead caught by an arresting net on an offshore platform. It is still too early to say which approach is better, but this achievement shows that catching up does not have to mean copying. The same goal can be reached through a completely different engineering path.

What surprised me even more was that it took only about a year for the Long March 10B to go from formal project approval to its maiden flight and successful recovery. Of course, that does not mean the entire project was built from scratch within a year. It was supported by decades of technical experience accumulated through China’s Long March rocket program. Even so, the speed of development was astonishing.

LandSpace’s Zhuque 3 also came very close to succeeding last year. In December 2025, Zhuque 3 completed its maiden flight and attempted to recover its first stage. The rocket successfully carried out several critical maneuvers during its descent, but an anomaly occurred during the final stage and it was unable to land successfully. Although the recovery attempt ultimately failed, it still validated many important technologies and provided valuable experience for future attempts. This is often how engineering progress happens. It rarely jumps suddenly from zero to one. Instead, the final barrier is crossed through a series of attempts that come progressively closer to success.

I remember reading Elon Musk: Tesla, SpaceX, and the Quest for a Fantastic Future in 2016, and one passage from the biography left a particularly deep impression on me. After every failed launch, engineers had to comb the ocean for debris, retrieving fragments of the rocket piece by piece. They would then examine the wreckage and flight data to determine exactly what had gone wrong. SpaceX grew through this process of constant review, modification, and repeated experimentation. I found this deeply inspiring because they had no way of knowing whether what they were attempting was even technically feasible, and they also had to endure constant doubt and ridicule from more experienced people in the industry. The first three launches of Falcon 1 all failed. It was not until September 28, 2008, that the fourth launch finally reached orbit successfully. By then, SpaceX was running out of money. If the fourth attempt had also failed, the company might not have survived.

Since starting my own business, I have listened to many podcasts and read many books. Over time, I have come to realize that building a great company does not necessarily require being the first person to enter a new field. Being first can certainly provide advantages in technology, branding, and market recognition, but it also means bearing the enormous cost of proving that the engineering is feasible, educating the market, and finding a workable business model. Those who enter later may start behind, but they can build on what others have already validated, identify which directions are promising, avoid unnecessary detours, and focus their resources on a different technical approach, a better business model, or a market pain point that others have overlooked.

Whether someone starts first or joins later does not determine who will ultimately succeed. Innovation is not limited to doing something that has never been attempted before. It can mean being the first to make something work at an engineering level. It can mean finding a unique path to commercialization. It can also mean solving a long standing problem at a lower cost or with greater efficiency. What moved me most about the Long March 10B was not that China had finally created another SpaceX. It was that the achievement demonstrated how a later entrant can respect the direction validated by those who came before while still finding a path of its own.

Perhaps entrepreneurship works the same way. What truly matters is not whether you were the first to start, but whether you can understand the problem clearly, choose the right path for yourself, and gradually turn uncertainty into reality through repeated experimentation and reflection. When the rocket was caught by the net at sea, it was not only a returning first stage that had been captured. It was also the result of years of accumulated knowledge, persistence, and imagination.
