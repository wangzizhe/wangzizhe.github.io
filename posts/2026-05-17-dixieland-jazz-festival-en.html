Dixieland Jazz Festival: Those Magical Moments Connected by Music

This week, Dresden welcomed the International Dixieland Festival, the largest traditional jazz festival in the world. Since my first visit in 2023, this year was already my fourth consecutive year at the festival. After hearing so many performances over the years, I gradually figured out what kinds of bands and styles I enjoy the most, so my schedule this year was very focused. But in the end, what fascinates me and what I enjoy the most is still that irreplaceable live atmosphere. It simply makes me happy.

This year felt especially magical. At times, I almost felt like a star, with interactions happening everywhere. During Dan Popek’s piano performance, my girlfriend was invited on stage to play with him, even though she had never really touched a piano before. Dan quietly told her to just play the black keys, and I explained to her that in many keys, if you stick to the black keys, it is actually quite hard to go wrong. In the end, the result turned out surprisingly well. During the intermission, I talked to him and noticed that on the back of his CD there was an arrangement of Rachmaninoff’s Prelude in C-sharp minor, Op. 3, No. 2. I found that absolutely wild, because I simply could not imagine how that piece could be turned into jazz. Then he casually told me, “I’ll play it for you in the second half.” Haha, that was really fun.

Another band, Cottas Club from Portugal, also pulled me onto the stage in the middle of their performance. Usually I am the one watching other people bring the house down, but this time it was finally my turn. When I watched the video afterwards, I still felt how insanely exciting it was. The atmosphere in the room was just incredible. I was singing and dancing with no hesitation at all, very naturally, very smoothly, as if I had simply slipped into that state. After I got off the stage, several people came up to ask me about it. Some even thought I knew the band, and someone asked whether I had been hired specifically for the interaction. Haha, it was all very surreal.

The performance I was most looking forward to on the second evening was The Busquitos from the Netherlands. This band has such a distinctive style, although I still cannot really say what style it is. It feels like they have turned music into comedy. Especially the violinist. For the first time, I realized that an instrument could actually perform a sense of comedy. That exaggerated, playful, slightly mischievous feeling was so striking that it genuinely amazed me. The whole band is built around interaction and performance, and they interacted with us quite a lot as well. There were also many fun little details, such as the saxophonist’s shirt saying “super saxy.” Those small touches really showed their personality.

Later, we went to hear Peter Glessing & Band. This old man was truly fascinating. His hairstyle was interesting, the way he talked was interesting, and the most shocking thing was that he somehow made the clarinet and saxophone sound like a suona! And he played with such force. You could completely feel the love for music running through his body. The drummer in the band was his son, and the cellist had been working with him for over forty years. What moved me the most was that although he was clearly no longer young, after playing for several hours straight, his energy had not dropped at all. His stamina, his spirit, and his level of commitment were all astonishing. By the time he finished, it was already past midnight. When we were leaving, we happened to run into him downstairs while he was loading his instruments into the car. He even came over to talk to us, and after chatting for a bit, he gave us a hug. He really was such a lovely old man.

The following days became even more magical. People started recognizing me everywhere. Even two days later, an auntie stopped me on the street and showed me the video she had recorded of me interacting with the Portuguese band. And to me, this was almost a lifesaver. Because of the widespread iPhone recording issue at the time, the video I had taken had no usable sound at all, only a high-pitched electric noise. I quickly asked the auntie to send me her video, and later, during editing, I extracted the audio from her recording and put it back onto my own footage. At that moment, it really felt as if the video had come back to life. It was simply magical.

From my first time at the Dixieland Festival to this year, it has already been four years. Looking back, what has stayed with me the most has never been one particular song, but those strange and wonderful connections between people. Last year was the same. Sunshine Brass handed me an instrument and told me I only needed to shake it along with the rhythm, so I actually ended up performing with them. Later during the intermission, one of the young guys in the band came over specifically to talk with us and take photos. He was also the son of one of the band members. There was another band we really liked as well. Their style was mainly Boogie Woogie, with a bit of rock mixed in. During the break, the keyboardist and the bassist also came over to chat with us.

That feeling really has a kind of magic to it. Of course, the music itself matters, but very often, what truly stays in your memory are those moments when people suddenly become connected to one another. A hug, a joke, an unexpected invitation onto the stage, a video that was almost lost but somehow brought back to life. These things slowly become the brightest parts of the memory. In comparison, classical concerts can sometimes feel a bit “stuffy.” Everyone sits quietly, keeps a certain distance, and even the applause is restrained. But the Dixieland Festival is different. It lets you walk into it without even realizing it, take part in it, and eventually become a part of it yourself. And I guess that is exactly why I have kept coming back for four years in a row.
