50 minutes 26 seconds

When I saw the results of today’s humanoid robot half marathon in Beijing Yizhuang, I was genuinely shocked. Just think about last year, the best robot took 2 hours, 40 minutes, and 42 seconds, and the overall performance was, frankly, a bit of a mess. This year, HONOR’s “Lightning” finished the race in just 50 minutes and 26 seconds.

I even went out of my way to check whether this was the same HONOR that makes smartphones, and it turns out it is. I had no idea they were also building robots. According to reports, nearly 40% of the robots completed the race autonomously this time, and the organizers applied a 1.2× time penalty to those that were remotely controlled.

What struck me is that this isn’t just about the improvement of a single robot or a single company. It reflects rapid progress across the entire industry, including the supply chain and all upstream and downstream players. The pace of change is astonishing. China’s supply chain has become so strong that in Shenzhen, you could assemble a fairly decent humanoid robot in just a few weeks. I keep coming back to the same feeling, shock, and more shock.

I have always believed that competitions like this are incredibly meaningful. When teams bring their robots onto a real course and compete, it exposes problems, encourages exchange, and ultimately pushes the entire industry forward.

Looking at these results, my immediate reaction is that the pace of development in AI and embodied intelligence has already gone beyond what I can imagine. If we, as humans, do not seriously think about our future, large-scale replacement by AI and robotics may become inevitable. It reminds me of the wave of layoffs in China’s state-owned enterprises in the 1990s, the recent shutdowns and job cuts in German industry, and the ongoing wave of efficiency-driven layoffs in Silicon Valley. I listen to a lot of podcasts, and from interviews with people across different industries, I have gradually come to realize something unsettling. Even in top-tier Silicon Valley companies, many AI researchers are already worried that they could be replaced by AI and lose their jobs within the next one to two years. That realization is deeply shocking.

The impact of AI is fundamentally different from anything we have seen before. In the past, once you beat other candidates in an interview, you more or less secured your position. As long as your performance was not too poor, or you were not at the very bottom in a company with forced ranking, your job was relatively stable. You were competing with other people, and once you won, the position was yours. Even if someone more suitable existed, replacing you was not easy. But in the age of AI, it is no longer just competition between people, the position itself can be replaced. Even if you are already in the role, if that role can be automated at scale, you are no longer safe. The old logic that holding a position means stability is being broken. The way human society organizes work and collaboration is being fundamentally reshaped. And according to a report released earlier this year by Anthropic, this is only the beginning. In many industries where AI can already replace a large share of tasks, the actual replacement process has just started.

It is not that entire professions will simply disappear. More often, work that used to require ten people may soon require only one. Many jobs will not be fully replaced by AI, but will instead become AI-assisted, with far fewer humans needed.

I believe that, for individuals, opportunities will largely come from a few directions:

  1. Work on AI and embodied intelligence;
  2. Do work that is extremely difficult to quantify;
  3. Do work that involves extensive human interaction;
  4. Invest in AI and embodied intelligence–related industries;
  5. Engage in hands-on manual work.
Beyond these areas, many jobs are becoming increasingly fragile. Of course, no one really knows exactly what the future will look like. But one thing seems certain, change is accelerating, and the time we have to adapt is shrinking. Rather than waiting for change to happen to us, it makes more sense to move closer to it, understand it, and even participate in it. Those who keep adapting to the future may not always succeed, but they are far less likely to be left behind and more likely to stay in the game.
