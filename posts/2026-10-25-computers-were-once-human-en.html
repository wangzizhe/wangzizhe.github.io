Computers Were Once Human. What About Programmers?

There is an interesting pattern in the history of computing: many tasks performed by machines today were once jobs done by people.

Take the word "computer," for example. Today, we think of a computer as a machine. But before electronic computers existed, a computer was a person whose job was to perform calculations. Fields such as astronomy, navigation, and engineering required extensive computation, and organizations employed human computers to carry out tedious calculations using established methods.

Then electronic computers arrived. Tasks that once took human computers days to complete could now be finished by machines in seconds. People no longer needed to perform every calculation themselves. They only needed to tell the machine what to calculate.

Interestingly, the same pattern has repeated itself several times within computing.

1. From Computation to Databases

Early computers could perform calculations quickly, but managing data was still difficult.

Initially, data was typically stored in files. To find a particular record, programmers had to write code to read through files and search for the relevant information. Hierarchical and network databases later made data management more systematic, but programmers still needed to understand how records were connected and specify the paths used to access them.

In 1970, IBM researcher Edgar F. Codd introduced the relational database model. Query languages such as SQL were developed in the years that followed. Instead of worrying about exactly how data was stored or retrieved, programmers could simply describe the data they wanted.

Of course, databases did not eliminate every performance concern. Programmers still needed to understand indexes and data structures. But relational databases shifted much of the work previously handled by programmers to the database system itself.

In this process, the programmer's job shifted from describing how to describing what.

2. From Machine Language to Natural Language

The evolution of programming languages follows a similar pattern.

Early programmers had to write machine instructions directly. Assembly language came next, followed by higher-level languages such as FORTRAN, C, Java, and Python. Each new level of abstraction allowed programmers to worry less about low-level details. For example, performing a simple addition in machine language required dealing with specific instructions and data locations. In a high-level language, you could simply write a + b. The compiler would translate that expression into instructions the machine could execute.

Programmers were still programming, but they no longer had to handle every low-level step themselves.

Today, AI-assisted programming is driving a similar shift. In the past, building a website meant designing data structures, writing backend APIs, and implementing frontend interfaces. Now, more and more of this work can be delegated to AI through natural language instructions.

This does not mean programmers are no longer important. Requirements still need to make sense, systems still need to be secure, and code still needs to be reliable. But much of the implementation work that once belonged to programmers is gradually moving to machines.

Computer was once a human occupation before becoming the name of a machine. Could Programmer be going through a similar transformation today?

3. The Same Story Beyond Computing

If we look beyond computing, we find that this pattern is hardly unique.

In the early 19th century, the Jacquard loom began using punched cards to control weaving patterns. Complex weaving processes that once required continuous human control could now be carried out by machines following encoded patterns. The human role gradually shifted from controlling individual movements to designing the patterns to be woven.

In the 20th century, numerical control machines emerged. Traditional machine tools required workers to control the movement of cutting tools directly, while numerically controlled machines could follow programmed instructions. Later, with the development of CAD and CAM technologies, engineers could design the geometry of a part and let software plan the machining paths.

In 1979, VisiCalc, an early spreadsheet program, appeared. Previously, when a financial analyst changed a number, many related calculations might have to be redone manually. Spreadsheets allowed users to define relationships between cells, with the software automatically recalculating results whenever the data changed.

More recently, cloud computing has brought similar changes. With Kubernetes, an engineer can declare that a service should always have three running replicas. If one replica fails, the system attempts to restore it automatically and decides where the replacement should run.

These technologies may seem unrelated, but they share a common pattern: instead of carrying out every step themselves, people increasingly describe goals, rules, and constraints.

Machines take on more of the work, while humans operate at increasingly higher levels of abstraction.

4. The Boundary Between What and How

Looking at the history of technology, a clear pattern emerges.

At first, humans performed calculations, with machines providing assistance. Later, humans described algorithms, and computers executed them. Then, with SQL, humans described the data they wanted, and databases determined how to retrieve it. Today, people are beginning to describe software requirements in natural language, leaving AI to work out the design and implementation.

Each generation of technology turns details that humans previously had to manage into internal processes handled by machines.

This also shows that what and how are not fixed categories. What humans must explicitly describe today may become something machines handle automatically tomorrow. As technology advances, people keep handing over implementation details to machines and turning their attention to higher-level problems.

So, as AI continues to develop, where will the boundary between humans and machines ultimately lie?

One intuitive answer is that humans decide what to do, while machines figure out how to do it. But that answer may no longer be enough. AI can not only help us complete tasks, but also identify problems and formulate goals. For example, suppose you ask an AI to help increase a website's revenue. After analyzing the data, it might discover that the real problem is not a lack of features, but that too many users abandon the registration process. Rather than simply proposing an implementation, it changes the problem we were trying to solve in the first place.

At that point, the machine is already participating in defining the what.

Taking this a step further, if AI becomes better than humans at predicting market changes, evaluating personal strengths, and recommending career paths, some decisions traditionally made by people may also be delegated to AI.

But this raises three distinct questions: Is the machine capable of making a decision? Does it have the authority to make that decision? And who is responsible for the consequences? These questions do not necessarily have the same answer. Even if an AI can accurately calculate the financial benefits of a business strategy, that does not mean it should have the authority to decide how much employees must sacrifice. Some decisions involve more than calculation. They also involve values, interests, and choices.

The future boundary between humans and machines may therefore be defined less by what and how, and more by capability, authority, and responsibility.

5. What Is Left for Humans to Do?

Looking back, technological progress has not simply eliminated human work. It has continually changed the kinds of problems humans need to solve.

Human computers no longer need to perform endless arithmetic. Programmers no longer need to write every machine instruction. Database developers no longer need to manually determine every data access path. And now, AI is taking on an increasing share of programming work.

We can imagine this trend continuing. Perhaps one day, a person will only need to describe an idea, and a machine will build the entire product. Eventually, machines might even propose ideas worth pursuing on their own.

But that does not mean humans will simply sit back and wait for the results.

Technology can help us find ways to achieve our goals, and it can even help us discover new ones. But being better at prediction and calculation does not automatically give machines the right to decide what everyone should value. More importantly, humans may not want to hand everything over to machines. Creating, exploring, experimenting, and making mistakes are sometimes experiences we value for their own sake.

Perhaps the question we should really be asking is not what machines will never be able to do, but what we will still want to do ourselves, even when machines can do it for us.

From Computer to Programmer, machines are steadily taking over work that humans once performed. And every time machines take on a new kind of work, humans get another opportunity to reconsider what they truly want to do, and why they want to do it.

Perhaps that is the most important question left to us as technology continues to raise the level of abstraction.
