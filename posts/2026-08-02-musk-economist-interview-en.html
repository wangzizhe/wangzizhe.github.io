Elon Musk’s Interview with The Economist

This week, I watched Elon Musk’s full interview with The Economist. The interviewer was Zanny Minton Beddoes, the magazine’s editor in chief. The final half hour in particular was absolutely fascinating.

I see Musk as someone who is willing to say what he genuinely thinks, and many of his views strike me as highly pragmatic. I generally do not enjoy listening to European mainstream media figures or politicians, because so much of what they say is shaped by political correctness and by narratives rooted in ideology and abstract values. Many of these discussions feel detached from reality, as though they were taking place in a vacuum.

During the final half hour, they debated a wide range of issues concerning Europe, including whether Britain might eventually descend into civil war. That prediction may be somewhat exaggerated, but the real issues behind it are immigration, refugees, and social integration in Europe. Ten years on, it is fair to say that Europe’s refugee policy at the time has largely failed. Europe accepted a large number of refugees, yet many have integrated into society far more slowly than originally expected. A significant proportion have remained outside the labour market for years and continue to depend on state benefits, which are ultimately funded by taxes paid by other people who work hard for a living. Europe’s economy and competitiveness are now in sustained decline, even if many people living in Europe have not yet felt the full impact. Sometimes you need to step outside the system and look at it from the outside before the change becomes truly visible. Had some of that money been invested in technology, education, and social development instead, Europe might be in a better position today.

This is why dealing with real world problems solely through the lens of values can so easily backfire. If a refugee was already illiterate or poorly educated in their home country, how can anyone expect that person to integrate easily into a completely unfamiliar society with a different language and culture? Integration under those circumstances is extraordinarily difficult. If someone remains unable to integrate, find employment, or enter mainstream society for years, frustration can build, and that person may eventually become vulnerable to extremist ideas.

Accepting immigrants is the right choice, but people cannot all be treated as though they were interchangeable, with decisions made solely on the basis of abstract values. To put it plainly, the United States often makes strategic choices and influences the direction of regional events, while Europe is left to bear the consequences and clean up the aftermath. The refugee crisis, the energy crisis, and the war in Ukraine have all reflected this dynamic to varying degrees over the past decade. American policy and geopolitical strategy have helped shape the course of events, but Europe has borne most of the direct pressure from migration, higher energy prices, and rising security costs. All of these factors have compounded one another, leaving Europe in an increasingly difficult position.

Another issue Musk raised was that many refugees and immigrants from Muslim societies place religion above almost everything else. There is a Chinese saying, “When in Rome, do as the Romans do.” It means that when people move to a new place, they should make an effort to understand and adapt to local customs and social norms. But for those who believe that religion takes precedence over secular law and local culture, such adaptation does not come easily. They may regard their own values and ideology as universally valid and see no reason to change simply because they have moved to another country.

When large numbers of people from Muslim societies enter Europe, and some of them place religious beliefs above secular rules, serious conflicts with the surrounding society are likely to emerge. I am not saying that all Muslims think this way. But based on Europe’s experience over the past decade, some clearly do regard their own values and way of life as universally applicable. In that respect, they are not so different from Europeans who believe that their own values represent universal values. When two groups live in the same place and both believe that their values are universally correct, conflict becomes difficult to avoid.

By contrast, I believe Germany’s more recent effort to attract Indian talent is a relatively sensible direction. This includes people entering Germany through skilled employment and professional channels, as well as the large number of Indian students who study in Germany and later enter the local labour market. In general, these people have already received a good education and have a much clearer path into further study or employment. They are often able to enter society and the labour market more quickly than refugees with limited education and insufficient language or professional skills. Germany’s current approach, which places greater emphasis on educational background, skills, and employment potential, is far more reasonable than the refugee policy of ten years ago, which lacked adequate selection mechanisms and supporting infrastructure.

Another point Musk made that I found particularly interesting was that the number of people who die from heat in Europe each year may exceed the number who die from gun related causes in the United States. I looked into it myself and found that there is indeed data supporting that conclusion. It was genuinely eye opening, because European media frequently criticise America’s gun problem, yet rarely discuss Europe’s large number of heat related deaths with the same intensity.

The most compelling part of the final half hour was not limited to the refugee issue. The two also argued about freedom of speech in Europe, public safety, far right political parties, trust in traditional media, and whether Musk has any right to involve himself in European politics. Beddoes questioned why Musk, who has not lived in Europe for years, should describe Britain as a country on the verge of losing control to hundreds of millions of followers. Musk responded that traditional media organisations have also spent decades interfering in the politics of other countries, and that selective reporting and ideological bias have caused them to lose the trust of a large part of the public. Today, public hostility towards mainstream media has reached a level that would once have been difficult to imagine.

Overall, I believe Europe’s mainstream media, its politicians, and more fundamentally European society itself need to wake up. If decisions continue to be driven by ideology and abstract values while costs, capacity, and real world consequences are ignored, Europe’s position will only continue to deteriorate. Europe needs to escape its narrow view of the world and develop a better understanding of how the world works today. No set of values can be separated from real conditions and assumed to apply automatically to every country, every society, and every person. What works should be studied and adopted. What does not work should be discarded. Only by starting with facts, acknowledging problems, calculating costs, and adjusting policies according to results can Europe have any real hope for the future.

Finally, I should make one thing clear. I criticise Europe so often because I love this place, just as I frequently criticise China. I criticise them precisely because I care. Otherwise, I could simply remain indifferent.
