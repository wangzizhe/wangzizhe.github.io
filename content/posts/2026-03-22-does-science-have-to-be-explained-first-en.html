Regarding the gradual marginalization of Europe in today’s AI competition and many other technological waves, I discussed a more obvious reason in my January 31 blog post: a general lack of enthusiasm for new technologies. But in recent years, through my observations in the German academic community, I have come to believe that there is a deeper reason: a methodological preference, or even an obsession, with formalism and interpretability. Many researchers in computer science and software engineering are accustomed to requiring that systems be modelable, provable, and interpretable. As a result, in their view, deep learning, lacking a clear internal mechanism, should not even be considered science. The problem, however, is that this seemingly “stricter” standard may in fact be systematically excluding a class of approaches that are already producing real capabilities.

Around New Year, I read Yann LeCun’s autobiography, and his debate with Judea Pearl over deep learning really left a strong impression on me. Pearl has explicitly argued that deep learning resembles a kind of “alchemy,” precisely because it lacks interpretability and causal structure, while LeCun has repeatedly pushed back against this view. My takeaway was not that one of them is right and the other is wrong, but rather that this reflects a classic methodological divide: one side insists that science must begin with interpretability and causality, while the other is willing to accept that useful capabilities can emerge from data and experience. The real issue is that if one insists on the former position, one must also provide an alternative path that achieves comparable results. Otherwise, this stance becomes less a viable research program than a normative rejection.

From this perspective, it becomes clear that this is not unique to deep learning, but rather a recurring pattern in the history of science. Many fields that are now regarded as “hard sciences” went through a similar early phase: they first proved effective in practice, while people still could not fully explain why they worked. Flight was achieved before aerodynamics had fully matured. Steam engines were already transforming the world before thermodynamics was established. Telegraphy and other electromagnetic technologies were in use before Maxwell’s unified theory. Darwin, too, proposed evolution without knowing anything about genes or DNA. At the time, these fields were often questioned because their theories were incomplete and their mechanisms were still unclear. But history did not end by rejecting those directions. On the contrary, it was precisely the practices that had already proven themselves and continued to work that forced theory to catch up, revise itself, and become more complete. In other words, many major breakthroughs in history did not follow the pattern of “first explain, then succeed.” More often, they looked more like “first succeed, then be explained.”

In more abstract terms, this reflects a long-standing tension between experimental science and theoretical science. Theoretical science tends to build self-consistent systems from a small set of assumptions, while experimental or engineering approaches are primarily concerned with whether something actually works in the real world. These two paths are meant to be complementary, yet in practice they are often implicitly ordered: theory comes first, experiment follows as validation. However, historical experience shows that this ordering is often reversed at critical moments. Put differently, in many cases phenomena are observed first, while theory follows only gradually, often with a significant delay. In this sense, treating interpretability as a prerequisite for science may seem like a philosophical stance, but in fact reflects a neglect of historical experience. A more concrete example can be found in embodied intelligence, such as robotics and autonomous driving. In these areas, mainstream approaches have already, to a large extent, moved away from hand-written rules. In some systems, such rules have even been removed entirely. By contrast, data-driven methods have demonstrated clear advantages in both performance and generalization, while extensive rule-based design often becomes a bottleneck for further improvement. Under these conditions, continuing to prioritize interpretability while insisting on hand-crafted rules likely means falling behind the current direction of technological development.

Deep learning sits precisely at the sharpest point of this tension. It is true that in many cases it lacks clear internal explanations and does not fit neatly into traditional causal frameworks. Yet at the same time, it has demonstrated stable and scalable performance across a wide range of tasks. Take the ImageNet competition as an example. After the introduction of ResNet in 2015, object recognition error rates dropped below human level (around 5% for humans versus about 3.6% for ResNet). This is why the problem was, in many ways, “solved” from an engineering perspective. Under such circumstances, insisting that something is “not worth studying because it is not interpretable” is not really a defense of scientific standards. It is a refusal to engage with reality.

Moreover, humans themselves are not fully interpretable systems. Our decisions are not always traceable, and we do not possess a complete causal model of our own cognition, yet this does not prevent us from being considered intelligent agents. Holding deep learning to a much higher standard of interpretability than we apply to ourselves is therefore difficult to justify as a consistent scientific position. It looks more like a double standard.

I have therefore come to see the current debates around deep learning as a manifestation of a more fundamental question: must science begin with interpretability? If we look at history, the answer is not particularly complicated. Science often does not begin with understanding, but with undeniable effectiveness. The role of theory is not always to lead, but often to compress and reconstruct what has already proven successful. In this sense, many criticisms of deep learning today are less about deep learning itself than about an older debate over the relationship between experimental and theoretical science.

To dismiss something as “not science” simply because it is not yet interpretable is, in my view, a serious logical error. More regrettably, this way of thinking is not uncommon among computer science researchers in Europe. One could even argue that many researchers across different fields in Germany are still deeply influenced by a traditional manufacturing mindset, making it difficult for them to adapt to the technological transformations currently underway. It implicitly assumes that only phenomena explainable within current theoretical frameworks qualify as legitimate scientific objects. Yet the history of science shows repeatedly that our theoretical frameworks are never complete; they are constantly challenged, revised, and even overturned by new phenomena and capabilities. If a domain has already demonstrated stable, reproducible, and scalable performance, then it has at least become a scientific problem in its own right, rather than something to be excluded from science. Refusing to study it does not make it disappear. It simply leaves the opportunity to understand it to others. From this perspective, using “lack of interpretability” as a reason for dismissal is not a sign of greater rigor, but rather a way of using the limits of existing theory to constrain the possibilities of future science.

Sometimes I feel that Europe’s relative decline in this technological wave may partly stem from exactly this: applying seemingly more “rigorous” standards that end up excluding directions which are not yet understood but are already producing real capabilities, effectively imposing unnecessary constraints on itself. I often feel that what is called rigor may simply be another way of missing the moment. For myself, this is a caution worth keeping in mind: when facing something new, do I distance myself because it is not yet explainable, or do I engage with it because it is already effective? The answer to that question may determine not just a research path, but whether one misses an entire era.
