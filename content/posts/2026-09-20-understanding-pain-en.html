I bench pressed on Monday, and by Tuesday I had a headache on the left side of my head. Sometimes the pain seemed to reach my left eye as well. At first, I didn’t think it had anything to do with my neck. Then I found a very specific tender spot near the hairline on the left side. Turning my head hurt, and pressing on the spot hurt too. But strangely, when I pressed on it, the spot itself hurt more while the pain in my head and eye actually eased. Ten or fifteen minutes of heat could sometimes make the headache noticeably better.

I had always assumed that pain was pretty straightforward: if something hurts, that must be where the problem is. This experience made me realize that the body does not always work that way.

A small irritated area on the left side of my neck could somehow make the entire left side of my head hurt, sometimes even my left eye. I later learned that this is called referred pain. Sensory signals from different parts of the body are not perfectly separated once they enter the central nervous system. Some of them converge in the spinal cord, brainstem, and higher levels of sensory processing, which means the brain cannot always identify the original source of a signal with perfect accuracy. The irritation may start in the neck, while the experience generated by the brain is simply: “My head hurts.” In other words, we do not have direct access to the true internal state of our bodies. What we experience is the nervous system’s interpretation of that state.

Muscle tissue does not independently produce the experience of pain. What actually happens in a muscle is mechanical strain, inflammation, metabolic changes, microscopic tissue damage, and activation of nociceptors. These receptors send signals through the nerves to the spinal cord, brainstem, and brain, but those signals are not pain yet. Pain is the subjective experience that emerges after the nervous system processes them. Put simply: the tissue provides the signal; the brain produces the pain. The nervous system also takes into account context, previous experience, attention, emotion, fatigue, and sleep before producing the final experience of pain. In that sense, the state of the tissue is the input, and pain is the output. Pain is not something that simply exists inside a muscle. It is the nervous system’s interpretation of what the body is going through.

It turns out that even something as simple as “Where does it hurt?” is not nearly as simple as I once thought.
