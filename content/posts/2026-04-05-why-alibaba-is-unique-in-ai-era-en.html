I’ve been thinking about a question lately: how is it that a company like Alibaba, so complex, so large, and so layered as a platform business, has managed to stay at the forefront of foundation models even long after Jack Ma stepped away from day-to-day operations? The more I think about it, the less trivial it seems.

Because at its core, foundation models are not something that large companies can naturally excel at. They resemble a long, high-uncertainty technological war that requires sustained capital burn, tolerance for delayed returns, and the ability to keep technical teams from being constrained by legacy business logic. This is not something that can be achieved through normal organizational inertia.

From experience, this tends to be a game dominated by two types of companies. One is startups, which are lightweight, low-burden, and face lower costs of failure, where founders can go all in and teams can move quickly around a single objective. Companies like OpenAI (GPT), Anthropic (Claude), xAI (Grok), DeepSeek, Moonshot AI (Kimi), MiniMax, and Z.ai (GLM) fall into this category. The other type is large companies where founders have reasserted strong control, such as ByteDance (Seed), Google (Gemini), and Xiaomi (MiMo), where AI has been elevated back to a core strategic priority under direct founder influence.

Alibaba fits neither category. It is not a startup, nor is it a case of a founder returning to take the helm. Yet under these conditions, Alibaba has consistently produced top-tier models such as Qwen. The real question is not why it produced a strong model once, but why it has been able to keep doing so without falling behind.

The answer comes down to two key factors.

First, Alibaba has not truly transitioned into a fully professional-manager-led company. While Ma Yun has stepped back, the core leadership represented by Tsai Chung-Hsin and Wu Yongming is itself part of the founding circle. This means that ultimate strategic control still resides within the founder system. On that basis, AI has been redefined as the company’s primary strategic direction. This is not rhetorical. It is reflected in real resource allocation, organizational focus, and long-term commitment. But this only explains why Alibaba is willing to invest. It does not fully explain why it has been able to sustain execution.

The second and more critical factor is that Alibaba has deliberately preserved a startup-like operating mode within its model efforts. It has not allowed large model development to be fully absorbed into the company’s processes and bureaucracy. Instead, it has maintained a focused objective, high technical priority, rapid iteration cycles, and continuity in direction, without being repeatedly disrupted by short-term financial pressures. This is crucial. On the surface, competition in foundation models is about compute, data, and parameters. In reality, it is about organizational state. Many large companies do not lack resources, but their processes, coordination overhead, and short-term evaluation systems gradually smooth out high-uncertainty, long-cycle projects.

This is where Alibaba differs from many others. Tencent has capital and scenarios, but Hunyuan has yet to enter the top tier; Baidu was early but has repeatedly started early and arrived late; Microsoft is undoubtedly strong, but its in-house model MAI has yet to demonstrate its true capability; Meta, once in the top open-source tier with Llama 3, has clearly fallen behind by Llama 4; Huawei’s Pangu model was initially within the mainstream field of competition but quickly dropped out of the core tier, and in 2025 it was even questioned as being a wrapper over Qwen. In the end, these companies either lack sustained execution or struggle to maintain long-term commitment.

Alibaba’s uniqueness lies in its ability to combine the resources of a large corporation with an internal technical enclave that operates much like a startup. This allows Qwen’s development cadence to resemble that of a team still fighting at the technological frontier rather than a conventional big-tech project, with continuous iteration, continuous releases, and continuous forward momentum. When these two elements come together, a third advantage emerges. Alibaba is not just capable of building models, but also possesses cloud infrastructure, enterprise customers, real-world scenarios, and a developer ecosystem that can rapidly amplify its model capabilities.

In other words, Alibaba is not simply building a model. It is using the density of a large company’s resources to scale a model organization that still operates with startup DNA. This sets it apart from many others. Some companies lack the will for sustained investment, others lack execution efficiency, and few manage to combine both. Alibaba has constructed a rare combination with founder-level strategic direction alongside a startup-like execution model within the organization. The result is a path that differs from conventional approaches. It is not driven by a founder’s return to the front line, nor by the extreme singular focus of a startup, but by using large-scale resources to support a team that still operates under startup logic.

Alibaba is now facing a real test. With the departure of Qwen’s head, 32-year-old Lin Junyang, on March 4, this technical enclave has, for the first time, shown a visible crack at the leadership level. For a frontier project that relies heavily on key technical leaders, such changes are rarely just personnel shifts. They are a direct test of organizational capability. Alibaba has already proven that it can cultivate a startup-like model team within a large corporate structure, but what remains to be seen is whether this capability can be decoupled from individuals and sustained over time. This will determine whether Qwen remains on its trajectory, or whether its success turns out to be only temporary.
