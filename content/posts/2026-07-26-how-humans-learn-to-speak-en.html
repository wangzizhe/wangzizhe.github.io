I was in a bus recently when I noticed a very young child sitting nearby. He clearly could not speak yet, but he kept babbling earnestly at the adults around him. Nobody could understand a word, but that did not seem to bother him in the slightest. He was having a great time. I watched him for a while, and a question suddenly occurred to me: How exactly does a child learn to speak?

When we are young, we tend to think of speaking as something that simply happens. A child reaches a certain age and somehow graduates from meaningless babbling to “Mom” and “Dad,” and then from a handful of words to complete sentences. But when you revisit the question as an adult, especially now that large language models have entered the picture, you begin to realize that there is nothing simple about it. Before young children can express anything meaningful, they spend a great deal of time producing all kinds of sounds. I used to think they were just making noise, but I later learned that they are actually practicing. Every time they vocalize, they are experimenting with how to control their tongues, lips, throats, and breathing, much like a beginner pianist practicing basic finger movements or a toddler learning to balance before taking their first steps. What is even more interesting is that babies everywhere produce similar sounds during their earliest stages of development. No matter where they are born, they tend to find sounds such as “ma,” “ba,” and “da” relatively easy to make because of the way the human vocal apparatus is built.

But being able to produce sounds is not the same as being able to speak. The truly remarkable question is how a child eventually learns that the sound “mama” refers to their mother. Nobody sits down with a dictionary and explains, one entry at a time, “This person is your mother, and these two syllables form a noun that refers to her.” The real process is much messier. The mother appears every day, holds the child, feeds them, and talks to them. Other people repeatedly say things like, “Mommy’s here,” “Go find Mommy,” or “Let Mommy hold you.” After hearing these patterns thousands or perhaps tens of thousands of times, the child’s brain gradually notices that the sound “mama” consistently appears alongside a particular person. Eventually, the sound acquires meaning. Learning the word “apple” works in much the same way. The child sees a red or green object while an adult points at it and says “apple.” They hold it and feel its weight, bring it close enough to smell it, and bite into it to experience its sweet or tart flavor. The sound, shape, texture, smell, and taste repeatedly occur together until they become bound into a single concept in the child’s mind.

Seen from this perspective, the human process of understanding the world has a strongly statistical quality. We constantly observe our surroundings and notice which things tend to occur together: a certain sound repeatedly appears with a certain person, dark clouds are often followed by rain, a cup falls when we let go of it, fire burns when we touch it, and falling down hurts. The world enters our minds little by little through these repeated associations. From there, it is hard not to think about today’s large language models (LLM). People often say that an LLM is merely predicting the next token, and there is certainly some truth to that. A large model processes enormous quantities of text, learns relationships between words, notices which terms tend to appear together, and identifies what kinds of phrases usually follow particular sentences. Through repeated prediction and correction, it develops an internal model of language.

But if you think about it carefully, human beings do not seem all that sophisticated during infancy either. Most of what a baby does each day is listen, watch, imitate, experiment, make mistakes, and adjust their judgments in response to feedback. Today they call a dog a cat. Tomorrow an adult corrects them. The day after that, they finally begin to understand that although cats and dogs both have fur and four legs, they are not the same kind of animal. The whole process resembles the continuous updating of an internal model. That leads to another question: What is the greatest difference between humans and LLMs? Many people would say that humans understand, while LLMs merely perform statistical prediction. I used to find that distinction convincing, but the more I think about it, the less clear the word “understanding” becomes. When a child knows that an apple is a red fruit, is that understanding, or is it the result of having encountered many apples? When a child knows that a falling cup might shatter, is that understanding, or simply the accumulation of repeated observations? Could much of what we call understanding emerge gradually from statistical patterns across a vast number of experiences?

Of course, there are still enormous differences between humans and today’s LLMs. The most important one, in my view, may not be intelligence but embodiment. We have eyes, ears, hands, and feet. We become hungry, cold, and hurt, and we fall when we lose our balance. We know that fire is hot not merely because we have read the sentence “fire has a high temperature,” but because we have felt its heat and may even have been burned by it. We know not to squeeze a paper cup full of water too tightly, not because we have studied materials science, but because our fingers can sense the softness of the cup, the weight of the water, and the way the paper begins to deform under pressure.

Every day, we marvel at the fact that AI can write code, compose essays, solve problems, and carry on conversations. Yet if we placed a robot in an ordinary home, it might require a great deal of training simply to pick up a paper cup full of water without crushing it. When humans perform this task, we do not calculate the maximum pressure the cup can withstand, nor do we solve equations describing the movement of our finger joints. We simply reach out and pick it up, while our muscles, vision, and sense of touch coordinate automatically. Countless past experiences of holding cups, touching objects, misjudging our grip, and correcting our movements have already built an extraordinarily efficient “intuitive physics engine” inside the brain.

A baby throws a toy from the table and watches it hit the floor. An adult picks it up, and the baby throws it again. To an adult, this can be mildly irritating. To the baby, it is almost an experiment. They are observing what happens when they let go, why different objects make different sounds when they land, why some bounce, why some break, and why others roll away. These apparently meaningless repetitions are actually binding the visual, auditory, tactile, and motor systems together. The child has never studied Newton’s laws, but they are gradually developing an intuitive understanding of gravity, collision, hardness, softness, and space.

By comparison, today’s LLMs are like brilliant bookworms who have read an enormous library but have spent their entire lives locked in a room without ever touching a physical object. An LLM knows that “apple” frequently appears alongside words such as “red,” “sweet,” and “fruit.” It can even write down the equations that describe an apple falling. But it has never actually held an apple, felt the texture of its skin, or experienced the pain of one landing on its foot. LLMs primarily learn about the world through language, whereas human language is connected to the physical world from the very beginning. The words we use are linked not only to other words, but also to bodies, emotions, actions, and concrete experiences. This may help explain why large models can sometimes be confidently wrong. A model can generate an explanation that sounds entirely plausible without possessing a body that allows it to test that explanation against reality at any moment. It knows what a true sentence is likely to sound like, but it may never have felt the weight of the world behind those words.

This raises another question: Why are humans so efficient at learning about the world? An AI system may need to examine a vast number of images before it can reliably recognize an animal, while a child may see only a few cats before identifying them across different colors, sizes, and poses. Human beings seem capable of generalizing from remarkably small amounts of data. One important reason is that we do not begin from scratch. The human brain is not a completely blank slate. Over the course of evolution, living organisms have repeatedly encountered space, gravity, predators, food, and members of their own species. Individuals who were better at detecting edges, recognizing faces, judging motion, and sensing danger were more likely to survive and reproduce. After countless generations of selection, we are born with mental structures and tendencies already suited to processing the physical and social world.

In other words, a newborn baby may know almost nothing, but they arrive with a set of initial conditions shaped by evolution. They do not need to invent vision from scratch, rediscover that objects tend to move continuously, or spend decades learning to pay attention to human faces. Experience only needs to fine-tune structures that already exist. In a sense, human learning is so efficient because evolution has already done a great deal of the training for us. Every one of us is born carrying a set of pretrained parameters written by countless generations of life.

At this point, an obvious idea arises: If the human system is so effective, why not simply reproduce it as closely as possible in AI and robotics? Give machines artificial eyes, artificial muscles, and structures modeled on the human brain, and perhaps they will come to understand the world as we do. But the history of humanity’s attempts to imitate nature suggests that it may not be so straightforward. Centuries ago, when people wanted to fly, the most obvious approach was to study birds. Birds flew with wings, so people strapped wings onto themselves and tried to imitate the flapping motion. Leonardo da Vinci designed flying machines with flapping wings, and many later inventors attempted to take flight using mechanical wings. Most of those attempts failed.

The problem was not that birds were incapable of flying. The problem was that people had copied their appearance and movements without fully understanding the principles behind flight. Birds possess specialized skeletons and muscles with an extraordinary power-to-weight ratio that mechanical devices cannot easily reproduce. What eventually carried humans into the sky was not a machine that looked more like a bird, but fixed wings, propellers, and jet engines. An airplane does not flap its wings, but it exploits the underlying principles of lift and airflow. Successful biomimicry is often less about copying an outer form than about understanding the mechanism beneath it. Velcro does not resemble an entire burr plant; it borrows the structure of the tiny hooks found on the burr’s surface. The front of Japan’s Shinkansen trains does not reproduce an entire kingfisher; it borrows the streamlined shape of the bird’s beak as it enters water with minimal disturbance. Failed imitation often copies movement. Successful imitation copies principles.

That is also why I do not think the machines of the future necessarily need to become human. Researchers working on embodied intelligence are trying to give AI systems vision, touch, and the ability to move through real environments, allowing them to learn through action and feedback. This is an important direction, but machines may not need bodies identical to ours, nor do they necessarily need to reproduce the firing pattern of every human neuron. Human knowledge is largely trapped inside individual brains. One child may learn from dropping a cup, but another child still has to repeat the experience. Machines could possess a very different advantage: if one robot in Tokyo learns how to pick up a paper cup, that experience might be shared almost instantly with thousands of robots around the world. A machine could also perform millions of trials in a simulated environment in a single day without breaking real cups, damaging its body, or consuming years of physical time.

A car does not need four legs to outrun a human, and an airplane does not need to become a bird in order to fly. In the same way, machines may not need to imitate humans in order to understand the world. Yet there may still be something fundamentally similar in how humans and machines learn. Both infants and large models must discover patterns in experience, update internal models in response to errors, and compress overwhelming amounts of information into structures that help them predict what will happen next. Humans hear language, observe actions, and experience pain as they gradually construct a model of the world. AI systems process text, images, and feedback as they form internal representations of their own.

At the same time, humans and machines may be traveling along entirely different paths. Human understanding grows out of embodiment, evolution, social relationships, and the limited experiences of a mortal life. Machine understanding may emerge from massive datasets, rapid replication, virtual simulation, and forms of perception we have not yet imagined. The next time I see a child babbling or repeatedly throwing a toy onto the floor, I may no longer think of it as meaningless noise or misbehavior. What I will see is a small living being, equipped with structures shaped by a long evolutionary history and endowed with touch and emotion, using very little data to connect sounds, the body, and the world. The child is practicing more than speech. They are learning what people are, what objects are, what the self is, and what exists beyond it.

Meanwhile, a very different kind of learning is taking place inside servers, laboratories, and robotic bodies around the world. Humans and machines are both trying to understand the falling cup. We see it begin to fall, reach out to catch it, hear it hit the ground, and may even feel upset when it shatters. A machine might first encounter gravity in written text, then learn trajectories from video, and finally use a mechanical hand to practice gripping the cup with exactly the right amount of force. We may be drawing on similar patterns, or we may be following entirely different routes. Either way, we are all moving toward an understanding of the world—we are simply getting there in different ways.
