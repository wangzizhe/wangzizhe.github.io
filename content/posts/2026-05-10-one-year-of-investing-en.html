A year has passed since I started my investment journey. As someone committed to a long-term approach, the market's ups and downs over the past year have given me more than just portfolio gains. They've taught me a lot about mindset and self-awareness.

My portfolio grew 35% over the past year. I'm mainly invested in US and Chinese tech stocks (about 15 individual stocks + 3 ETFs). Among them, CATL and Google doubled. Nvidia rose 90%, just shy of doubling. And back in early October, NIO briefly doubled as well.

First, investing comes down to human nature, and the hardest part of human nature is patience.

Patience isn't just gritting your teeth and holding on. It's waiting with confidence. Not chasing highs isn't about a lack of desire. It's knowing that valuations have already priced in too much future growth. Not panicking during selloffs isn't about a lack of fear. It's about having checked and rechecked that the fundamentals haven't changed. This past year taught me that patience and independent thinking are two sides of the same coin. Without independent thinking, patience becomes blind stubbornness. Without patience, independent thinking is just talk. As long as the fundamentals are solid, it's only a matter of time before the price follows. That's easy to say. Actually believing that takes a few market cycles.

Second, everyone can be right, but everyone's "right" has its own context.

Every day, I read news and opinions. The bulls have their data. The bears have their logic. They're all making valid points from their own time horizons, positions, and perspectives. But the key is that their reasoning may not apply to you. Your capital, your time horizon, your risk tolerance. These are completely different from anyone else's. So here's what I learned this year: listen carefully, but don't follow blindly. All outside opinions are just input. The final decision is yours alone.

Third, look for undervalued stocks, but you need your own valuation anchor.

Last year, when almost everyone was bearish on Google and NIO, I built my positions. Undervalued assets are almost always controversial. Without controversy, there's no real opportunity. But controversy and uncertainty are precisely where opportunity comes from. Right now, I think Xiaomi and Adobe are in a similar spot. Of course, only time will tell if I'm right or wrong.

Fourth, Wall Street pros focus on the short term because that's how their game is set up.

Retail investors' biggest advantage isn't better information or sharper analysis. It's time. Institutions live quarter to quarter. Fund managers fear redemptions. Analysts are measured month by month. They have no choice but to obsess over short-term earnings, spending, and quarterly numbers. Retail investors don't. As long as your money isn't borrowed and you're not forced to sell, you can afford to wait three to five years for a company to prove itself. That's a huge advantage, so obvious that most people somehow miss it.

Fifth, think upstream. That's one way to find certainty.

The rise of LLMs: the surest beneficiaries aren't which app becomes the winner, but upstream capacity like GPUs and memory, because every single query burns through them. For AI Agents, the bottleneck shifts to CPUs and storage. The brutal price war in Chinese EVs: no matter who comes out on top, CATL as the battery supplier is always the one selling shovels. Late last year, when I went to buy a hard drive for my Mac and noticed prices had spiked, I just thought it was expensive. Only later did I realize that was an upstream supply-demand signal. If I could learn to make these connections earlier, that would mean I've truly internalized this way of thinking.

Conclusion

Before I started investing, I never really understood why so many retail investors chase highs and sell into lows. I also wasn't sure how my own mindset would hold up during a sharp downturn. A year later, it turns out my temperament is pretty steady. If anything, I understand even less now why people chase rallies and panic during selloffs. For me, a market selloff is exactly the time to add to quality names. If a good stock looks expensive, I'll open a small starter position and add more if the price pulls back.

In the past year, I learned to read financial statements and joined several earnings calls. Through continuous reading and research, I've slowly developed a much deeper understanding of certain companies and industries. That deeper understanding helps me think about the future more clearly. And these are things you only get by actually owning the stock. Honestly, that's one of the most important reasons I invest. Having some skin in the game forces me to truly understand a business and its industry.

One year is a very short time in the grand scheme of investing. The 35% return owes something to luck. What really gives me a sense of solid footing is that I've started to build a framework for thinking that belongs to me. I don't feel invincible after a big rally, and I don't doubt everything after a pullback. At its core, investing is about exchanging sound judgment for the value that time builds. And time is the one thing that treats everyone equally. The only difference is whether you have the patience to stand on its side. Next anniversary, I hope to look back just as calmly and honestly.
